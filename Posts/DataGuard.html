Building DataGuard: Teaching an AI to Explain Broken Data

A star schema, a set of deliberately broken rows, and the question of whether a language model actually helps you fix them faster, or just feels like it does.

Most data quality tools are good at one thing: finding what is wrong. They are much worse at the second half of the job, telling a person what that means and what to do about it. I wanted to know if an AI explanation layer sitting right after detection actually closes that gap, or if it is just a nicer looking version of the same raw output. So I built a small pipeline to test it on myself, honestly, with a real measurement at the end instead of a guess.

Starting with data I could trust, because I broke it on purpose

The project starts with a synthetic e commerce star schema: customers, products, dates, and a fact table of five thousand order line items. I generated all of it myself with a fixed random seed, and I planted three specific problems inside it: fifty orders with no customer attached, twenty five orders pointing to a customer that does not exist, and twenty five orders with an impossible quantity of five thousand units.

Planting the bugs myself mattered. It meant I always knew the right answer, which is the only way to check whether a detection tool, or an AI explanation of that tool, is actually correct rather than just plausible sounding.

Detection: Great Expectations reading the data, not guessing at it

I built the schema properly with dbt, joining a raw fact table to its dimensions with left joins specifically so the broken rows would survive the join instead of quietly disappearing. Then I pointed Great Expectations, a data validation framework, at the finished table with five plain rules, things like every order needs a customer, and quantity should fall in a sane range.

It found exactly what I had planted. Fifty missing customers, twenty five orphaned ones, twenty five bad quantities, and it correctly left two other checks passing, confirming it was not just failing everything by default.

Somewhere in here I found a genuine bug in my own bug detector: an older version of the validation library returned its pass or fail result as a numpy boolean instead of a plain one, which silently turned into the text string False when written to a file, a string that Python treats as true. A real failure was quietly being counted as a pass. Fixing it meant explicitly casting the value before saving it, and it is the kind of small, easy to miss thing that makes me trust a pipeline more once I have actually hunted one down inside it.

Explanation: giving Claude the failure, and the reason the check exists

Once Great Expectations flags something, the raw output is a JSON object full of counts and column names. Technically correct, not something you would hand to a non technical teammate. So the next step sends only the failed checks to Claude, along with a short plain English description of why each rule exists in the first place, and asks for three things: what went wrong, the likely cause, and a suggested next step.

That last part, the description, turned out to matter more than I expected. Without it, the model only knows a column failed a rule. With it, an explanation reads like something a colleague who actually looked at the data would write, not a reworded error message.

The part most people skip: actually measuring it

It is easy to assume plain English explanations help. I wanted a real number, even a small one, rather than a confident sounding guess. So I timed myself, honestly, reading each of the three real failures two ways: once as raw JSON, once as Claude's explanation, typing a one line diagnosis each time and letting the clock run.

FailureRaw JSONExplanationChange
Missing customer42.8s13.7s68% faster
Orphaned customer49.9s54.5s9% slower
Bad quantity72.6s9.8s86% faster

Two of three improved a lot, especially the quantity check, which needed the most manual reasoning to work out from raw numbers alone. But one check came out slower with the explanation, and I am not going to smooth that over. It was likely the first item shown in that round, which probably added a few seconds of simply getting oriented again, independent of the format. With a sample size of one person and one trial, that is a real limitation, not something a bigger average should be allowed to hide.

Averaged out, diagnosis time dropped by fifty three percent. I trust that number more because I am also showing you the one case where it did not hold.

What actually building this taught me

  • Dimensional modeling and transformation as code with dbt, on a schema small enough to reason about completely.
  • Working with a real third party validation library across two different versions of its own API, which is closer to real engineering than a tutorial that only ever shows the happy path.
  • Using a language model as one component inside a deterministic pipeline, where its only job is translation, not decision making.
  • The discipline of measuring a claim instead of just making it, and reporting the result even when it is mixed.

The full project, including the code for every step above, the bug fix, and the benchmark itself, is on GitHub. If you build something similar and get a different result, I would genuinely like to hear about it.
